reading #3

This reading allowed me to think about possible reasons why the graphic novel format was the chosen medium. The graphic novel has unique affordances, such as perspective and representation. Throughout the women’s accounts, the graphic novels easily switch between the first and third point of view. The medium imposes a fluid temporal space. The narration is given in the past tense while the action and dialogue are presented in a present linear time. The illustrations can depict difficult content, such as the abuse enacted on these women in frank or abstract ways. At the same time, the emotions of the drawings reduce the potential exploitation in representing such traumatic events. The affordances of the medium stood out during the fourth story, where the reader audience can see Tetteh’s ghost of her ex-husband along with the version of Tetteh in the graphic novel. The representational aspect of the graphic novel frequently confronts the audience with the “fourth wall”. Another key element of the visual storytelling is the multi-media digital format. The photographs and videos of the workers involved with women’s healthcare were initially jarring. The impact of when the videos and photographs were deployed was relieving to me, as they were positioned as an epilogue to a specific story. The illustrations of these women’s struggles are juxtaposed by the interviews of the staff who contribute to improving the conditions of women’s healthcare in Sierra Leone. The graphic novel goes beyond representational storytelling towards an informational documentary project.

The complex disentanglement of fear and shame was a constant theme throughout the chapters. Some of the fundamental fears concerning women’s healthcare crisis rise from memory and stigma. It is interesting for the graphic novel to frame these stories of medical crisis between the shadow of the Ebola outbreak and the COVID pandemic in the future. The lack of medical support during the Ebola crisis pushed families to have reservations about seeking help outside their communities for any kind of emergency.
However, throughout the chapters, one constant healthcare challenge to women’s lives was misogyny. I connected with the women of Sierra Leone despite our different access to healthcare infrastructure. Despite our own healthcare system and presumed education in the United States, women and girls’ own awareness of our bodies are also limited by broader notions of misogyny whether it be external or internal. One great example of the disentanglement of traumatic memory and shame was in Memuneta’s chapter, when the women in the medical facility share their stories without the fear of shame. In contrast to social isolation and patriarchal ignorance, community support and knowledge flourished. Through the graphic novel, the stories of the Sierra Leone women validate memory and create community with shared experiences.
