reading #4

The article, through multimedia and coding, presents the author's vision of an imagined end to decades-long “dogma of deterence”. The article's strength lies in how it communicates the existential fear through illustration and the digital affordance of scrolling. The scrolling can be interpreted as interactive or immersive action to engage with the content of the argument. In particular, zooming into the illustration of nuclear fallout gives the reader a safe but detailed point of view of a cataclysmic possibility.
The piece identifies the global subconscious fear of the atomic bomb as a “balance of terror”. To me it is more of a balance of aggression between the most powerful people on Earth. Despite the intention to fit the reader into the story about nuclear fear, the use of the scroll reinforces a passive audience to witness violence regardless of our will. Often the violence in this article is framed as being done from an unknown other, often relying on what people know based on geo-politics. In the later illustrations, the images of the nukes are sent off in one direction towards the reader and centering the core audience of the NYT, the US-American reader, under threat. Much of the“dogma of deterence”, was a reactive policy ethos to prevent attacks on the United States which developed as a world power post-WWII. Most importantly, deterence of atomic bombs could only be enforced after the fact of its use on the Japanese people, as if that was the price for a new tool to display the power of nations.

However, the disclosure and paranoia of nuclear weapons often resulted in escalation by all parties. The article emphasizes the propagation of nuclear bombs created between the US and the USSR, as well as the decreasing number of the bombs after the fall of the Soviet Union. To benefit the narrative of fear, the graphs in the article end with more bombs held in the Eastern hemisphere, without further explaining the history behind the context of the information presented. In these cases, a news article is not suited for a long and complicated series of events.
The biggest issue I had with this article was the use of illustration to depict a possible fictional nuclear attack on undefined people. At the core of the article's argument, it asks its readers to imagine with the help of illustration. The use of illustration is not my issue with this approach. Japanese society has been greatly affected by these displays of power and has been the topic of art and media, like the animation films Barefoot Gen and Grave of the Fireflies. Imagining violence is not the most impactful request. It may be because I am a history major, but I believe it is more important to remember the past, not to envision more violence. The most powerful people are not satisfied with human destruction at present levels. With the author's call to imagine, does that mean the memory of violence also is not enough?
